The AI Timeline Pyramid

Artificial intelligence did not appear overnight. It grew from ancient questions about thinking machines, through the rule-based programs of the 1950s to the 1980s and two winters of lost funding, to the data-driven revolution in machine learning and, finally, the generative models and large language models that many people now use every day. This pyramid stacks that story in layers: the wide base is the groundwork, each narrower layer is a more specialised and more capable stage, and the apex looks at what comes next. Select any event to see what happened, why it mattered and how it changed industry and daily life.

        Design plan

        The five planning decisions for the pyramid architecture, based on the 31 milestones in the research table. Each one is applied to the live page above: use the design view controls to see it.

        1. Layers

        Seven layers from the base up, plus an optional eighth for the future. The brief suggests five. The research table crowds toward the modern end, so language models, generative image models and AI for science each get their own narrow layer rather than one overloaded top layer. Layers group events by how the technology works, so they overlap a little in time.

        LayerNameWidthEvents (numbers from the research table)
        • Which layer? Before working systems: layer 1. Rules, logic or search: layer 2. Artificial neurons and learning from examples: layer 3. Deep networks trained at scale: layer 4. Transformers and language models: layer 5. Image-generation models: layer 6. Specialised systems for science and reasoning: layer 7. Speculation about the future: layer 8.
        • Order inside a layer. Left to right by date. Two events in one year are ordered by month.
        • Crowded layers. A layer with more tiles than fit one row wraps into balanced, centred rows (3 + 3, not 5 + 1). Width never changes, so adding events never changes the shape.
        • Layers follow technique, not date. The perceptron (1957) sits in layer 3 while LISP (1958) sits in layer 2. Turn on Connectors to see the zigzag.
        • The future layer. The research table has no future or AGI row yet. Add an event with "layer": 8 in data.js and the apex appears.

        2. Visual narrative

        • Width is accumulated groundwork. The base is 100 percent and each layer above is 8 points narrower, down to 52 percent (44 with the future layer).
        • Tone is depth. Fills run from deep slate at the base to pale blue at the top, like strata of rock.
        • A coloured edge is the subfield. Each tile also prints its subfield name, so colour is never the only clue.
        • Time reads upward. Turn on Connectors to thread every event in date order.
        • Events never move. Search and filters dim tiles, so people keep their sense of the whole shape.

        3. Tooling

        Chosen: an interactive HTML page (this page: HTML, CSS and plain JavaScript, with no framework or build step).

        Why: it is the only option on the list that gives full control over the pyramid’s shape, real buttons for every event, search and filtering, keyboard and screen-reader access, a layout that adapts to phones, and a free shareable link through GitHub Pages.

        • Canva: quick, but the layout is template-bound and detail views are separate pages.
        • Prezi: zooming hides the overview the pyramid exists to show.
        • Figma: excellent for sketches, but a prototype is not a real page with keyboard and screen-reader support.
        • Slides with links: a fallback if nobody can code.

        4. Interactivity

        • Select a tile (click, tap, Enter or Space) to open its detail card with the four-part note and a link to the source.
        • Move between events with Previous and Next, or the Left and Right arrow keys.
        • Close with the button, Escape or a tap outside. Focus returns to the tile.
        • Search and subfield pills dim non-matching tiles and combine with each other.
        • Share a link such as index.html#m20 to open an event directly.
        • Where the detail appears: a centred card on desktop, a bottom sheet on phones. Turn on Annotations, then open any event, to see each part labelled.

        5. Layout grid

        • Desktop: 12 columns, 24 px gutters, container up to 1120 px. Turn on Grid overlay to see them.
        • Layers: widths of 100, 92, 84, 76, 68, 60 and 52 percent (44 for the future layer), with 16 px padding, 6 px gaps and a 14 px corner radius.
        • Tiles: a fixed 160 px wide on desktop with 8 px gaps and a 6 px subfield edge. They flex on narrower screens.
        • Phones (under 760 px): 4 columns. Layers step in 16 px per level to keep the pyramid silhouette, and the card becomes a bottom sheet.
        • Very small phones (under 420 px): tiles go full width, one per row.
        • Connecting lines: a dashed amber thread joins events in date order, drawn between the layer fills and the tiles.
